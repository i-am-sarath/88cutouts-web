Layer order tells the viewer which object sits in front of another. If a tape scrap disappears behind the photograph it is supposed to hold, the arrangement can feel wrong even when all the colours match.

## Build a simple stack

Start with five roles: background, photograph, frame or paper mat, decoration, and text. These are conceptual roles, not a rule that every frame belongs above every photo. A solid paper mat goes behind the photograph; a transparent border may go in front.

![An exploded layer stack showing background, photograph, decoration and a separate text layer.](/guides/layers.svg)

Name the layers by purpose in your editor. `caption-date` and `tape-top` are easier to understand than `Layer 12 copy`, especially when you return later.

## Decide what each overlap means

Tape should cross both the photo edge and the surface beneath it if it is meant to look attached. A flower tucked behind a card should disappear at the card's edge. A sticker on top of the card should remain visible across that edge.

Choose one interpretation and maintain it. If half of an object behaves as though it is behind a photo and the other half appears in front without a reason, the depth becomes confusing.

## Keep text independent

Do not flatten text into the photo until you are ready to export. A separate text layer makes it easier to correct a spelling error, change a date or adapt the design to another shape.

Protect letter counters and punctuation from overlapping decoration. A small star across the centre of an `a` can change the word's appearance more than a large flower in an empty corner.

## Inspect silhouettes before shadows

Temporarily hide shadows and other effects. If the object boundaries are unclear without them, adjust position or layer order first. Shadows should reinforce an understandable arrangement, not disguise a confusing one.

Then add only the effects that fit the intended material. A flat printed sticker can sit flush with a card, while a raised paper scrap may justify a slight shadow.
