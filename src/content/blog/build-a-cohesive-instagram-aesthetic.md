"Aesthetic" as used on Instagram means something quite specific: a set of images that look like they belong to each other. Not identical, not repetitive — related. Most advice about achieving it starts and ends with recommending a preset pack, which is a bit like being told a house looks coherent because of the paint.

Cohesion comes from constraints. Here is a way to choose four of them and then largely stop thinking about it.

## Constraint 1: a palette of three plus one

Pick **three base tones** that sit close together in lightness, and **one accent** that does not.

The base tones are what most of each image is made of — the walls, the sky, the fabric, the table. Because they are close in value, images made from them look calm and related even when the subjects differ completely.

The accent is your only permitted contrast. It might be a colour (a single red object recurring), a tone (one genuinely dark element in otherwise light images), or a material (metallic against matte).

Some combinations that work, as starting points rather than prescriptions:

- **Warm neutral:** oat, cream, clay + one deep brown. Reads calm and expensive.
- **Cool minimal:** paper white, pale grey, soft blue + one black. Reads clean and editorial.
- **Blush:** blush, ivory, dusty rose + one burgundy. This is the [coquette](/blog/coquette-aesthetic-guide/) family.
- **Sage:** sage, cream, stone + one terracotta. Reads botanical without going full cottagecore.
- **Muted dark:** charcoal, slate, deep green + one warm gold. Works well in winter and for dark-mode-heavy audiences.

The test for whether a palette is working: open your last nine posts as thumbnails and squint. If you see one colour family with occasional punctuation, it is holding. If you see a fruit salad, it is not.

**The most common mistake** is having two accents. Two accents cancel each other out — neither reads as special, and the image acquires a second focal point that competes with the first.

## Constraint 2: one light direction and quality

This is the constraint almost nobody sets deliberately, and it does more work than the palette.

Light has two properties that read instantly: **direction** (where it comes from) and **quality** (how hard the shadows are).

- **Soft, diffuse, directional** — a north-facing window, an overcast day. Gentle shadows with soft edges. Reads calm, intimate, expensive. The default for most "aesthetic" feeds.
- **Hard, directional** — direct sun, a bare bulb. Sharp-edged shadows, high contrast. Reads energetic, editorial, summery.
- **Flat, frontal** — on-camera flash, ring light. Almost no shadow. Reads documentary, Y2K, party.
- **Backlit** — subject against the light source. Rims and haze. Reads dreamy, cinematic.

Any of these is fine. Mixing three of them in one grid is what makes a feed feel scattered, and no amount of colour grading fixes it, because you cannot grade shadow hardness away.

Pick one, shoot for it, and simply do not post the images that came out in a different light. This is the single highest-leverage constraint on the list and it costs nothing.

## Constraint 3: a subject range, not a subject

Feeds that post one thing get boring; feeds that post anything get incoherent. What works is a *range* — three or four recurring subject categories that people learn to expect.

For example: hands and objects, interiors, a recurring drink, and text-only cards. Or: outfits, close-up textures, sky, and food. The specific categories matter far less than there being a small number of them.

Two useful patterns:

**Alternate scales.** If your last post was a wide shot, make the next a close-up. A grid that alternates between broad and tight has rhythm; one made entirely of mid-distance shots is flat even when everything else is right.

**Include breathing posts.** Not every post needs to be a hero. A quiet texture, a piece of paper, an almost-empty frame — these give the grid pauses, and they are what makes the strong images feel strong by comparison.

## Constraint 4: one recurring motif

A motif is a small visible signature that appears in some but not all of your posts. It is the thing that makes someone recognise your image in a feed before they read the handle.

It can be almost anything:

- A specific object that keeps appearing.
- A consistent border or frame treatment.
- A hand entering the frame from the same side.
- A single recurring cutout or sticker.
- A hand-written caption style.

The cutout version is the easiest to control, because a transparent PNG placed at consistent scale and position is genuinely identical every time. A single [sun charm](/stickers/golden-sun/), a [torn paper scrap](/stickers/torn-kraft-paper-scrap/) in one corner, or a [small bow](/stickers/small-red-satin-bow/) at a consistent size becomes a signature after about five posts.

The rule is **appear in some, not all**. A motif in every single post becomes a watermark, which reads as branding rather than personality. Roughly one in three is about right.

## Applying the four constraints

The workflow, in practice:

1. **Write the four constraints down.** Literally, in your notes app. Palette, light, subject range, motif. This takes ten minutes and it is the whole strategy.
2. **Shoot more than you post.** Cohesion is mostly an editing discipline. If you post everything you take, your feed reflects the variety of your week rather than your decisions.
3. **Edit toward the palette, not toward a preset.** Most editing apps let you adjust individual colour ranges — pull oranges toward your base tone, desaturate the greens you did not intend to have. This is more effective than any preset because it responds to what is actually in the photo.
4. **Check in nines.** Instagram shows three across; nine is what a visitor sees before scrolling. Review your last nine as a block, not as individual posts.
5. **Let one post break the rules occasionally.** A feed with zero variation reads as a catalogue. One deliberate outlier per twenty posts adds life. The key word is deliberate.

## Stories and Highlights are part of it

The grid gets all the attention, but Stories are where most engagement actually happens, and Highlight covers are the first thing under your bio.

**Stories** can be looser than the grid — they are ephemeral, and rigid consistency there reads as effortful. But keeping the same palette and the same sticker vocabulary makes the transition from grid to Story feel continuous. Practically: use cutouts from one or two [categories](/) rather than picking a different style every time.

**Highlight covers** are the strongest cohesion lever per unit of effort, because there are only five to eight of them and they sit permanently at the top of your profile. A matched set — same background tone, same icon treatment, same scale — does more for a first impression than any three grid posts. There is a full method for this in [the highlight cover guide](/blog/instagram-highlight-covers-guide/).

**Your profile picture** completes it. It is small and always visible, so it should carry the palette rather than the detail. The [DP maker](/dp-maker/) is one way to produce one that uses the same cutouts as your Stories, so the whole profile reads as one system.

## Auditing a feed you already have

If you are not starting from scratch — and almost nobody is — the useful exercise is to work out what your constraints *already* are, since most feeds have accidental ones.

**Screenshot your last eighteen posts as a grid.** Two screens' worth. Look at it small.

**Then answer four questions about what you see, not what you intended:**

1. *What colour is this feed?* Name the two or three tones that recur. If you cannot name them, that is your finding.
2. *What light is this feed?* Count how many of the eighteen are soft, hard, flat or backlit. If there is a majority, that is your light constraint and you should adopt it deliberately.
3. *What is this feed about?* List the subject categories. If you get more than five, you have too many.
4. *Which three posts feel most like the account I want?* This is the most useful question on the list. Those three are your reference; write down what they have in common.

Most people discover they already have a partial system and are undermining it perhaps a third of the time. The fix is not a rebrand — it is deciding which of the existing tendencies is the real one, and stopping posting the images that contradict it.

**Do not delete old posts to force consistency.** Archiving history to make a grid tidy costs you the record of the account and gains a cosmetic improvement nobody will notice, because visitors judge by the top rows. Change what you post next; the grid corrects itself within about a dozen posts.

## Editing toward a palette, practically

The step that turns a decision into an actual feed, and it takes about ninety seconds per image in any editor with colour controls — Lightroom Mobile, Snapseed, VSCO or Instagram's own tools all suffice.

**1. Fix exposure and white balance first.** Everything downstream depends on the image being neutral to start with. If it is too cool, warm it until skin and paper look right, not until it looks stylish.

**2. Lift the blacks slightly.** Raising the darkest point a few percent produces the soft, slightly faded look most muted aesthetics rely on. This one adjustment does more to unify a set than any filter.

**3. Desaturate the colours you did not choose.** This is the key move and the one most people skip. If your palette is warm neutrals and there is a bright green plant in the corner, pull the green channel's saturation down. The plant stays; it just stops competing.

**4. Push the remaining hues toward your base tones.** Most editors let you shift individual colour ranges. Nudging oranges toward your chosen warm tone across every photo is what makes images shot in different rooms look related.

**5. Add a small amount of grain.** Two to five percent. It gives digital images a consistent surface texture, and texture reads as intentional.

**6. Save it as a preset.** Once you have a version you like, save the settings and apply them as a starting point — then adjust per image. A preset as a *starting point* is useful; a preset as a finish is where the incoherent results come from.

## What cohesion is not

A few things that get confused with it.

**It is not sameness.** Nine near-identical photos is not cohesion, it is repetition, and it reads as a stock feed.

**It is not a filter.** Filters harmonise colour and do nothing about composition, light quality or subject. A filter over an incoherent set produces a tinted incoherent set.

**It is not permanent.** Aesthetics can and should shift. The graceful way to do it is gradually — change one constraint at a time and let the transition happen over ten posts, rather than switching all four at once, which just reads as a different account.

**It is not the point.** A cohesive feed is a means of making your work easy to recognise and pleasant to look at. It is not a substitute for having something to post. Plenty of accounts with beautiful grids say nothing, and plenty of scruffy accounts are worth following. Constraints should make posting easier, not turn it into a production.

## The condensed version

Choose three base tones and one accent. Choose one light quality and shoot for it. Choose three or four recurring subjects and alternate their scale. Choose one motif and use it in about a third of your posts. Then edit ruthlessly and check your work nine at a time.

That is the entire method, and it works because cohesion has never been about having good taste — it has been about making fewer decisions, repeatedly.
