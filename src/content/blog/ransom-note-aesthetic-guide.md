Some visual styles are invented by designers. This one was invented by people trying not to get caught, and then borrowed by designers who liked how it looked. That origin still colours it — the cut-out letter is one of the few typographic devices that carries an emotional charge before you have read a single word of it.

## Where it comes from

The practical version predates the aesthetic one by a century or so.

Handwriting analysis became a recognised forensic tool through the nineteenth century, and by the 1880s courts in Britain, France and the United States were routinely admitting expert testimony on it. If you intended to send an anonymous letter — a threat, a blackmail demand, an accusation — your handwriting was now evidence. Typewriters were not much better: individual machines develop identifiable wear patterns, misaligned characters and inking quirks, and forensic examiners learned to match a document to a specific machine.

Cutting letters out of newspapers and magazines solved both problems at once. Mass-printed type has no individual signature. A capital R from a newspaper headline is identical to every other copy of that day's paper, which is precisely the point.

The technique appears in real cases across the twentieth century, and it appears constantly in fiction about them, which is how it became legible to people who have never seen a real one. By the time it reached popular culture it was already a *symbol* of anonymity rather than a method of achieving it.

## The punk turn

The interesting move happens in the mid-1970s, when the style stops meaning "I am hiding" and starts meaning "I reject your production values."

Jamie Reid's artwork for the Sex Pistols — the *Never Mind the Bollocks* sleeve, the *God Save the Queen* imagery — is the reference point most people have, and it is a deliberate appropriation. The cut-out letter carried connotations of threat and illegitimacy, and that was exactly the register punk wanted. It also had a practical dimension: cut-and-paste layout could be done on a kitchen table with scissors, glue and a photocopier, at a time when actual typesetting required a print shop and money.

That practicality is why the style spread through the fanzine underground so completely. *Sniffin' Glue* and the hundreds of zines that followed it used ransom-note headlines not just as a statement but because it was what you could do with the tools available. The look and the means of production were the same thing, which is unusual and part of why it feels authentic in a way that later imitations often do not.

From there it moved outward: hardcore flyers, riot grrrl zines in the early 1990s, rave flyers, and eventually into mainstream advertising whenever a brand wanted to borrow a bit of edge. That last stage is where a lot of the style's power got spent.

## Why it works, formally

Strip away the history and there is still a real design mechanism underneath.

**Irregular baselines create motion.** When each letter sits at a slightly different vertical position, the word has a bounce to it. Uniform baselines read as calm and institutional; broken ones read as hurried, handmade or agitated.

**Mixed typefaces break automatic reading.** Fluent readers do not process letters individually — they recognise word shapes. Mixing serif, sans, condensed and display faces within one word disrupts that shortcut and forces character-by-character reading. That extra fraction of a second is felt as friction, and friction reads as urgency.

**Physical edges signal a physical object.** Torn paper edges, drop shadows, slight rotation — these say "this was assembled from things" rather than "this was typed". On a screen full of clean rendered type, that materiality is what makes it stand out.

**High contrast per letter.** Because each letter comes from a different source, each one has its own foreground and background relationship. That produces a shimmering, uneven texture that pulls attention even at small sizes.

## Composing one that looks right

The single most common mistake is randomising everything. True randomness does not look hand-made; it looks generated, because hand-made things have *constrained* variation. A person cutting letters from magazines is limited by what was on the pages in front of them, works quickly, and unconsciously keeps things roughly level.

So the rules are about limits, not chaos.

**Limit the source palette.** Three to five distinct "sources" — think of them as three different magazines — repeated across the message, rather than a different look for every character. Real ransom notes repeat letterforms constantly, because the person had one newspaper.

**Constrain the rotation.** Between about −8° and +8°. Beyond that letters stop reading as pasted and start reading as scattered. Vary the amount, but keep every letter within the band.

**Constrain the baseline.** Vertical offsets of roughly ±10% of the letter height. Enough to break the line, not enough to make the word illegible.

**Vary size modestly.** A 20–30% range between the smallest and largest letters. Larger ranges look like emphasis rather than material variation, which changes the meaning of the word.

**Keep the spacing tight.** Cut-out letters were glued close together to fit on a page. Generous letter-spacing reads as digital and undoes the effect.

**Let a few letters overlap.** Two or three overlaps across a phrase, by a few pixels. This is what physical paper does and what software defaults never do.

## Where to use it, and where not

The style has a narrow but genuine range.

**It works for:** announcements that want energy, birthday and party graphics, band and event promotion, anything punk-adjacent, playful threats ("give me back my charger"), zine layouts, and short emphatic phrases inside an otherwise clean design.

**It does not work for:** anything longer than about six words, anything that needs to be read quickly, professional or institutional contexts, and any message where the recipient might genuinely be unsettled. That last one deserves a moment of thought — the style's origins are in intimidation, and while the reference is usually understood as a joke, it is not always received that way. A cut-out-letter message to a stranger reads differently from one to a friend.

**Length is the real constraint.** The friction that makes a three-word phrase compelling makes a sentence exhausting. If you find yourself composing a paragraph in cut-out letters, the style is working against you.

## Combining it with other elements

Cut-out lettering sits naturally with anything else that reads as paper. [Torn kraft scraps](/stickers/torn-kraft-paper-scrap/), lined-paper fragments and [washi tape](/category/paper/) all share the same material logic, and a phrase in cut-out letters taped down at the corners is a more finished-looking object than the phrase alone.

It sits badly with anything glossy or rendered. Chrome, gradients, glass effects and soft photographic blur all contradict the flat-paper premise. If you want a mixed look, let the paper elements own the foreground and keep the glossy ones as background.

For a Story, the practical arrangement is: photo background, one cut-out phrase at a readable size — bigger than you think, since it will be viewed for three seconds — placed inside the [safe zone](/blog/instagram-story-size-and-safe-zones/), and one or two paper elements to anchor it.

## Making one physically

If you want the real thing rather than a simulation, it is an hour's work and the results are hard to fake.

**Source material.** Magazines with varied typography are ideal — fashion titles, music press, supplements. Newspapers give you the classic look but limited size variety, since headline type sits in a narrow range. Junk mail is surprisingly good: catalogues and flyers use wildly inconsistent type because nobody is art-directing them.

**Cut, do not tear, the letters themselves.** Torn edges on individual letters look messy and reduce legibility. Cut close to the letterform, leaving one to two millimetres of background around it — that surrounding rectangle of paper is part of the look. Tearing is for the larger paper elements underneath.

**Collect more than you need.** The most common frustration is running out of a letter mid-word. English letter frequency is a real constraint: E, T, A, O and N appear far more than the rest, so cut extras of those and stop worrying about J, Q, X and Z until you need them.

**Lay out before gluing.** Arrange the whole phrase dry, photograph it, then adjust. Glue is unforgiving and repositioning tears the paper.

**Glue with a stick, not liquid.** Liquid adhesive wrinkles thin magazine paper almost immediately.

**Then scan or photograph it.** A flatbed scanner at 600 dpi is best. If you are photographing, shoot flat under soft even light — a window on an overcast day — with the camera directly overhead, and avoid your own shadow. Cut out the background afterwards using the method in [how to make transparent PNG stickers](/blog/how-to-make-transparent-png-stickers/), and you have a cutout with real paper fibre, real cut edges and real shadow, none of which any filter reproduces convincingly.

## Colour, which is usually got wrong

Digital versions of this style tend toward black letters on white paper, because that is what a newspaper looks like. Real cut-out notes are much more varied, because real magazines are.

Expect and use: white paper, cream paper, yellowed newsprint, coloured stock, reversed type (white letters on a black rectangle), letters cut out of photographs so the background is an image rather than a colour, and the occasional metallic or fluorescent from an advertisement.

The reversed-type letters are the most useful and the most often missed. Two or three white-on-black characters in a phrase of otherwise dark-on-light letters create tonal rhythm and stop the word reading as a uniform grey block. Real notes have them constantly, because headlines are frequently reversed.

The one rule: **keep the letters themselves either dark-on-light or light-on-dark, not mid-tone-on-mid-tone.** Each individual character has to carry its own contrast, because it cannot rely on the ones next to it.

## A note on legibility

Because this style deliberately damages legibility, it needs help elsewhere.

- **Size up.** Cut-out text needs to be perhaps 30% larger than clean type to read as easily.
- **Keep the contrast high** between the assembled phrase and whatever is behind it. A dark photo behind dark-printed letters is unreadable no matter how well composed.
- **Use it for the headline only.** Any supporting text should be in a plain, quiet typeface. The contrast between the two is part of the effect.
- **Read it back at thumbnail size.** If you cannot read your phrase when the image is a fingernail wide, nobody scrolling will either.

## The short version

The ransom note look is a hundred-year-old solution to a forensic problem that punk turned into a design language. It works because irregularity slows reading down and materiality stands out against clean screens. Compose it with *constrained* variation rather than randomness — a handful of repeated sources, small rotations, tight spacing, a few overlaps — keep it to a few words, and it still does exactly what it did in 1977.
