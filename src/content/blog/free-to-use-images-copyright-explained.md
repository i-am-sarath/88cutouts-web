This is one of the least glamorous things to understand and one of the most useful. The number of accounts that lose posts, get strikes, or receive an unpleasant email because someone assumed "I found it online" meant "I may use it" is substantial, and almost all of it comes from five terms being used as though they were interchangeable.

They are not. Here is what each one actually means.

*This is a plain-English explanation, not legal advice. Copyright details vary by country, and anything with money attached is worth a real opinion from a real lawyer.*

## The default is: you may not

Start here, because it reverses most people's intuition.

Under the Berne Convention, which nearly every country has signed, **copyright is automatic**. The moment someone creates an original work — a photograph, a drawing, a piece of writing — they own the copyright in it. No registration, no notice, no © symbol required. An image with no visible licence is not unlicensed; it is fully copyrighted and you have no permission.

So the question is never "is there anything stopping me?" It is "what permission have I actually been given?"

Two things follow that people find counterintuitive:

- **Being publicly visible grants nothing.** Publishing a photo on the open web is not abandoning rights to it.
- **Being uncredited grants nothing.** Not knowing who made something does not mean nobody did.

## The five categories

### Public domain

Works whose copyright has expired, been forfeited, or never applied. Genuinely free — you may use, modify, sell and redistribute them with no permission and no attribution.

**What is in it:** works by authors who died long enough ago (in most of Europe, 70 years after death; in the US, generally anything published before 1930), works produced by the US federal government, and works explicitly dedicated to the public domain.

**The traps:**

- **Term varies by country.** Something public domain in the US may still be in copyright in Europe, and vice versa.
- **A photograph of a public-domain work may have its own copyright.** This gets litigated — museums argue that photographing a painting creates a new copyrighted work; courts in the US have generally disagreed for faithful reproductions of flat art, and other jurisdictions differ.
- **Restoration and colourisation can create new rights** in the restored version.

### CC0

Creative Commons Zero: a tool a creator uses to place their own work as close to the public domain as their jurisdiction allows. Effectively public domain by choice.

Use it freely, commercially, modified, without attribution. Attribution is still polite and still good practice, but it is not required.

### Creative Commons licences

A family of licences that grant specific permissions in exchange for specific conditions. Four building blocks combine into six licences:

| Block | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **BY** | You must credit the creator |
| **SA** | ShareAlike — derivatives must carry the same licence |
| **NC** | NonCommercial use only |
| **ND** | NoDerivatives — no modifications |

So **CC BY** is the most permissive: use it anywhere, commercially, modified, just credit the author. **CC BY-NC-ND** is the most restrictive: personal use only, unmodified, with credit.

**Traps worth naming:**

- **"NonCommercial" is broader than "selling it".** Using an image on a monetised blog, in an advertisement, on a business's social media, or in anything that supports commercial activity is generally commercial use, even if no money changes hands for the image itself.
- **"NoDerivatives" is stricter than it sounds.** Cropping, recolouring, adding text and combining with other images can all count as derivative.
- **ShareAlike is contagious.** Using a CC BY-SA element in a composite work may require you to license the whole composite under the same terms.
- **Attribution has a form.** Best practice is title, author, source and licence, each linked. "Image: Google" is not attribution.

### Royalty-free

The most misleading term in the list, because it contains the word "free" and does not mean it.

Royalty-free means you **pay once and may then use the asset repeatedly** without paying per use. That is all it means. It is a pricing model, not a permission level. Most royalty-free images cost money, either individually or through a subscription.

Royalty-free licences also carry restrictions, commonly: no resale as a standalone asset, no use in a logo or trademark, no use in a "sensitive" context, and a cap on print runs or impressions. Read them.

### Free for personal use

A creator's own terms, and therefore whatever they say. Typically: use it yourself, in non-commercial contexts, without redistributing it or selling it.

This is the licence used for everything on this site. In plain terms: decorate your Stories, your chats, your notes, your printed cards, your school project. Do not resell the files, do not bundle them into a paid pack, do not pass them off as your own work, and get in touch before using them commercially.

It is also the licence category with the least standardisation, so "free for personal use" on one site and on another can mean noticeably different things. Read the actual page.

## The specific mistakes

**"I found it on Pinterest."** Pinterest indexes images hosted elsewhere. A pin is a pointer, not a licence, and frequently not even a pointer to the original source — images are re-pinned through many hops and the original creator is usually lost. Pinterest itself is a starting point for a search, never an answer.

**"It came up in Google Images."** Google indexes the web. Appearing in results says nothing about rights. Google's own tools let you filter by usage rights, which is useful, but the filter reflects metadata that is sometimes wrong.

**"I'll credit them."** Attribution is a condition of some licences and a substitute for none. Crediting a photographer whose work is fully copyrighted does not make the use permitted; it makes it a documented infringement.

**"It's only for a Story, it disappears."** Duration is irrelevant. So is audience size.

**"I changed it."** Derivative works are covered by the original copyright. Modification without permission is not a defence — it may add a second problem.

**"It's fair use."** Fair use (US) and fair dealing (UK, Canada, Australia, others) are real doctrines with real limits, and they are much narrower than internet usage suggests. They are fact-specific defences typically covering criticism, commentary, news reporting, teaching and parody, weighed against the effect on the market for the original. "I used it decoratively on my own post" is not among the recognised categories, and fair use is decided by a court, not asserted in advance.

## What happens if you get it wrong

Escalating, and most people never get past the first step:

1. **A takedown.** The rights holder files a DMCA notice or equivalent and the platform removes the post. Usually the whole story.
2. **A strike.** Repeated notices lead to account restrictions and eventually termination.
3. **A demand letter.** Some agencies systematically scan for uses of their catalogue and send invoices, often for far more than a licence would have cost.
4. **Litigation.** Rare for a personal account, real for a business.

The practical exposure is low for a personal Story and meaningfully higher the moment money is involved — a shop, a sponsored post, a product listing.

## Doing it properly

**Use sources whose licence you can read.** A site with a clear terms page is worth more than one with a bigger library and no terms.

**Record what you used.** A note with the source URL, the licence and the date takes ten seconds and answers any later question instantly.

**Attribute when required, in the required form.** Title, author, source, licence.

**When in doubt, ask.** Creators say yes more often than people expect, particularly for non-commercial use, and an email is free.

**Make your own.** The most reliable answer. [Making a transparent PNG sticker](/blog/how-to-make-transparent-png-stickers/) from a drawing or a photograph you took yourself removes the question entirely, and it is less work than it sounds.
