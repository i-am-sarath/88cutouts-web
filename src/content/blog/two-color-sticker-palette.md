A limited palette makes mixed assets easier to combine. You do not need to recolour every pixel into a matching swatch. Instead, choose which colour occupies most of the image, which colour draws attention, and which surface gives both room to work.

## Assign roles to the colours

For a practice design, use warm cream `#FFF4E5` as the base, muted blue `#557B91` for the main decoration, and coral `#D96C5F` for a small accent. Keep the caption dark enough to read independently of the accent colour.

![A large neutral swatch, a medium blue swatch and a smaller coral swatch illustrating unequal colour roles.](/guides/palette.svg)

The swatches are an example palette, not a rule about a particular aesthetic. A pale background with blue shells and one coral starfish is one way to use it; a blue photograph with a cream text panel is another.

## Match families rather than exact pixels

Photographic stickers contain highlights, shadows and local colour variation. Judge their overall impression at the size you will use them. A blue bow can belong in the palette even if its highlights are almost white.

If one sticker introduces a strong fourth colour, either replace it or decide that it is the new accent. Avoid treating every colourful object as a special exception; the palette stops providing structure.

## Change quantity before hue

When coral overwhelms the design, try shrinking the coral object or using it once instead of three times. You may not need a different coral. Visual dominance comes from area, contrast and placement as well as the colour itself.

Keep a neutral buffer around text. A caption placed across several colourful objects must fight each local contrast change, even if the palette is attractive as a set of swatches.

## Test a second arrangement

Duplicate the composition and swap the roles of blue and coral. Keep the layout unchanged. This makes the effect of colour distribution easier to see than changing everything at once.

Then view both versions in greyscale as a rough check of lightness separation. Greyscale does not prove accessibility, but it can reveal two differently coloured elements that have nearly identical tonal weight.
