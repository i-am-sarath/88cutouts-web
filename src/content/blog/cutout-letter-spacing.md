Cutout letters arrive with different paper shapes and transparent margins. Equal distances between their file boxes can still produce visibly uneven words. What matters is the apparent space between the letterforms people read.

## Start with a short word

Use a word such as `LOVE` as an exercise. Place the letters on a plain background and make them approximately equal in visible height. Ignore decorative rotation for the first pass.

![Paper letters placed along a common baseline, with gaps measured between the visible letterforms.](/guides/letter-spacing.svg)

Look at the gaps inside the word. A diagonal `V` can create a larger-looking wedge than a straight-sided letter, even when the boxes have identical spacing. Move the letters in small steps until the gaps feel similar in visual weight.

## Separate letter spacing from word spacing

For a two-word phrase, the gap between words should clearly exceed ordinary letter gaps. If the phrase reads as one long word, increase the word gap before spreading every letter apart.

Paper scraps can touch while the printed letters remain well separated. Conversely, two large white margins can make the word appear broken even when the image boxes touch. Inspect the lettering and the paper as two related shapes.

## Add variation after readability

Once the word reads clearly, introduce modest rotations or uneven heights. Make one or two letters playful rather than rotating every letter by the same alternating amount. Keep enough of a common baseline that the eye can follow the word.

For long names, reduce decorative variation. A familiar short word can survive more disruption than an unfamiliar spelling someone is seeing for the first time.

## Test the small version

Reduce the preview until the heading is roughly the size it will appear on a phone. If a gap closes, move the letters apart slightly. If a punctuation mark disappears, make it larger or use a clearer cutout.
